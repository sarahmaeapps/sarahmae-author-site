Chapter One

“And when did Mr. Rubix Senior acquire the patents for the Continuum Lattice technology?”

Only one hand raised out of all fifty students.

Elariel Vexley.

“Anyone else?” Mr. Britely asked.

Nobody even flinched.

“Fine,” he growled. “Elariel?”

Elariel squirmed in her seat as her cheeks flushed. She was always the first person to raise her hand, though she could never figure out why everyone else always seemed so lost when the answers literally seemed to jump off the page for her.

“Umm…” she stammered. “The patent was approved on Talver the seventeenth in Y-two-eighty-four.”

“Correct,” he said patiently. “And where did you find that date?”

“In the library, sir.”

“If the rest of you spent half the time researching your assignments…” Mr. Britely’s tone shifted, cold and blunt. “But that is, after all, why she is the only person passing my class.”

Elariel’s cheeks flushed as the entire classroom collectively turned to stare.

“And what else can we learn about Rubix Corporation’s Continuum Lattice from Elariel’s research?” He was looking directly into her eyes as he spoke.

“Well.” She squirmed in her seat, folding her feet under her chair, ankles locked, as her anxiety forced her left eye to shutter. “I don’t think…” She froze.

“It’s OK, child.” He tried to soothe her anxiety with a soft smile. “This is an eighth-grade classroom, it’s a safe place to discuss your thoughts. Go ahead and share what you found.”

Elariel swallowed hard as she prepared to elucidate for the classroom what she had read.

“I don’t think Rubix Corporation invented the Continuum Lattice, sir.” Elariel glanced around as the other children let out a flush of gasps. “I found a patent request from a company I’ve never heard of before.”

Mr. Britely was frozen at the front of the classroom, his lips parting more with every word she spoke.

“The letterhead said TerraPlat. They were a landscaping company. A small one; they had to borrow money from Rubix Corp to pay for the patent filing.” Elariel paused as a thought crossed her face. “I wonder if Rubix is why their patent request was denied.” Her speech picked up speed, and her head started to spin as her thoughts connected the dots at dizzying pace. “If Rubix blocked the patent, before the formation of the Union of the Five, that could explain how they went from one of the smaller corporations with government contracts to the dominance that we know them for today.”

Mr. Britely stood silent for a long minute before speaking. “And where did you find this information, young lady? I know the archives better than anyone, and I have not seen this information before.”

Elariel froze as fear gripped her, her heart rate speeding out of control and a bead of sweat dripping down her cheek. She knew the consequences of breaking the rules, and she was breaking nearly all of them.

“In the library,” she paused, dropping her eyes to the floor as her left eye blinked furiously, “in the basement.”

“The basement is restricted!” he gasped.

“I know, sir…” Fear stole the sentence before she could finish apologizing. People were known to disappear for making disparaging statements about one of the Big Five. “I’m sorry, sir. It’s just that the main library doesn’t possess enough data. I finished memorizing the archives on the main floor last year, so I sneaked downstairs.”

Mr. Britely considered her rationale for a full minute before opening his mouth. “Well, nobody caught you, and I did tell you that you were safe.” His eyes naturally strayed to the observation camera mounted in the back corner of the classroom. “And it’s too late now, so elucidate us on your observations, please.”

“Well,” she glanced around shyly, finally gaining control over her left eye, “I found the original patent request, and then I found a tome that talked about TerraPlat. Their own internal documents and memos show Continuum Lattice being originally created to level the ground, to make room for buildings and sidewalks and stuff. They referred to it as though it were a liquid that would flow and then level itself as it solidified.” She tipped her head as the next thought crashed into her consciousness. “But Rubix made it a solid as soon as it passes through the lens, so they could program it.” She paused again as her fear reclaimed the space her confidence had temporarily occupied. “If I read everything right, they didn’t invent anything but the layered printing process they use. I think they stole the technology itself…” Her eyes dropped to the ground.

“And how do you suppose they did that?” Mr. Britely’s interest was piqued by the new revelations as he forgot about the camera and started trying to pull the information from the child.

“They…” Elariel was struggling to process the thought that she was trying to communicate. “They used some sort of neutron cobalt bomb to kill — everyone.”

Elariel had forgotten herself as she spoke, abandoning her earlier caution for the thrill of discovery. Her speech sped up. “From what I found, they committed a genocide against nearly a million citizens and TerraPlat employees, then they moved in and took everything in secrecy inside The Wall.”

Mr. Britely turned to write on the chalkboard as he spoke, suddenly confident that he could direct the conversation back into the safe zone. “The Wall, TerraPlat’s founding corporate city, was said to be destroyed and rebuilt by Rubix after an accident with their Weaver Head.” He turned to make sure everyone was paying attention, only finding Elariel taking notes. “When the head exploded, it spread Continuum Lattice over everything inside the city, killing everyone and burying them under the Lattice. To my knowledge, the resulting lake of Continuum Lattice covered everything to five feet above the highest plateau, including the site where they first discovered the mineral needed to make the Iris lens, cutting off access to future extraction.”

Mr. Britely turned to the class as he put his chalk down. “Elariel, what does our federal law say about corporate takeovers?”

“The law states that when a conflict arises between corporations, the dispute is first reviewed by the courts. If they decline to take up the case, the conflict is permitted to be expeditiously resolved by the involved corporations by any means necessary.” Elariel recited the law word for word, with one exception.

“And what did you leave out, child?” he said impetuously.

She closed her eyes as the words formed in her mouth. “Up to and including any corporate or civilian casualties necessary.”

“Correct!” he barked as he turned back to the board and started scratching his chalk against it again. “No laws broken, no legal repercussions.” He locked eyes with Elariel as he finished. “No genocide.”

“But…” she protested.

“But nothing, child. They followed the laws that govern our great nation.”

“I think they are evil,” Elariel pouted. “I can’t imagine ever working for them!”

That conversation stayed with Elariel throughout the next five years of her schooling, her mind made up.

Rubix equals evil!

As she was drafting her valedictorian speech a few months before graduation, a new thought struck her for the first time. Citizens, herself included, in this society were valued only by the profit they brought to one of the Corporations.

And it was a successful strategy!

People pledge themselves to one of the Five, she thought, and in return the Corporation provides luxury housing, abundant food, the best medical care in the country, and ready entertainment, as long as the contract is fulfilled.

“It’s kind of beautiful if you look at it like that,” she said, more to herself.

“What’s that, honey?” Elariel’s mother called out from the kitchen.

“Oh, I was just working on my speech for next month, and I’m trying to figure out how to work my central thesis into it without sounding too obvious, and something occurred to me. I used to think the way our government is run was callous and cruel, but the more I’ve studied about it, the more idyllic the system sounds. I mean, the thought of working for one of them, the contract I mean, the benefits. And the starting pay is a whole lot more than anyone makes at one of those privately owned unincorporated places around town, and they’re far better than delivery, and…”

“Um, darling.” Her mom walked into the room. “You’re beginning to ramble again.”

“Huh? Oh, sorry. I was just thinking how nice it would be to be selected for one of the entry positions.”

“I hate to be the realist, but,” her mother wrung her hands as she tried to find the gentlest words to use, “it’s just that there’s only a few of those in our city’s allotment every year, and there are so many applicants.”

“I know, mom,” Elariel looked at her toes as she forced confidence into her voice. “But I am entering University with a four point seven grade average. That should count for something. Maybe I do have a chance, and yes, maybe I don’t. But there’s no harm in dreaming a little, is there?”

“Oh, my dear,” her mother smiled as she pulled her into a hug, “you have always been my little dreamer.”

“Nothin’ but smiles and sunshine from me, momma.”

“Now what about this speech?” Her mother turned her laptop screen to look and frowned. “I wish I could at least afford a better intelligence model for you, my child. With your brain and one of those new WeaverTech intelligence models, you might have had a real chance.”

“You did the best you could, momma.” Elariel pulled her mom’s chin to look into her eyes. “I am about to graduate at the head of my class. I am getting to write my own speech. Almost everyone else has to use a speech written by the censorship board. They aren’t even going to proofread mine! Do you know why?”

Her mom smiled. “Because my little girl is the smartest child this entire country has ever seen, that’s why!”

Elariel paused and rubbed her chin in mock sarcasm. “Hm. True!”

The two laughed as Elariel clicked on the file where her speech was saved. “Here is what I have so far, if you want to read it.”

“Please!” Her mother leaned in closer.

“Welcome, fellow graduates, friends and families of graduates. We hope you have found the ceremony so far efficient and pleasing, and we will do our best to make sure you enjoy your day with us.” Her mother paused. “Very appropriate opening statement, darling.”

She started silently reading the page as she scrolled, reading it to herself as though speaking to an audience while counting time. “Ah! I see what you were talking about, dear, and I think you did a perfect job tying that into the speech. In primary school we called it rulers, in secondary we called it oppression, in tertiary school we learned the consequences of our behavior. And we called it an Oligarchy. But today I suggest we call it by its real name, The Union of the Five. And give this system, within which we are mere cogs, the credit it deserves, because it is really none of these. This governmental system our founders have forged is the gold standard example of a true Meritocracy!”

Silence filled the room for a long moment before Elariel’s mother spoke. “Are you sure this is safe?”

“Of course I am, mom.” Elariel’s expression turned serious. “I have researched literally every single word. They have all been said without penalty. All of the inferences possible to get from these words in any arrangement have been proven safe by people before me.”

Elariel’s mother dropped to a whisper. “I just don’t want anyone hurt. You, me, the families of the kids in your class. You’ve been together since grade school, you know. If anyone finds offense in your speech, they will all be liable.”

“Would it make you feel better if I have Dean Withers proofread it for me?”

Her mother shook her head emphatically as a tear escaped the corner of her eye. “It would kill me if anything were to happen to you, my child.”

The next morning, Elariel was standing outside the school office before even the first administrator arrived, waiting for the Dean of Students.

“Elariel!” Dean Withers exclaimed with a smile. “Good morning, young lady. Have you been standing there waiting for me to show up?”

“Yes, ma’am,” Elariel said confidently. “I’m sorry to bother you first thing in the morning, but I was hoping you would have time at some point today to proofread my valedictorian speech for graduation?”

“Were you not informed that you do not need to have it approved? You are our best student in more than a decade, academically even longer than that. You have my trust.”

Her heels clicked on the tiled floor as she pushed her office door open and led Elariel inside. The room was a stark contrast to the maze of halls that riddled the school, all lacking in color and decor, while proffering a boardroom persona. The desk, imposing. A symbol more of authority than a piece of furniture. Dean Withers motioned for Elariel to take the seat opposite her chair.

“Thank you, ma’am,” Elariel blushed. “It’s really my mom…” Another beat. “I mean, I want to make sure. Umm…”

“You are clearly nervous about something, my dear,” Dean Withers said softly. “What did you want to say that makes it worth all this worry?”

“Well, that’s why I wanted you to read it, ma’am.” Elariel’s confidence returned, like gravity pulling her mind back to the present. “I’ve spent the last six months researching government and economics, social studies, and even Health Services. It’s all…” She paused, searching for the exact word she wanted. “Very elegant. We as children don’t see that until near moments before we start University. But it should be foundational our whole lives.”

“That’s a powerful message.” The Dean studied her thoughtfully for a long moment before continuing. “Let me take a look at it. Do you have it on disk?”

Elariel produced a mini-disk from her book bag, hesitating a moment before handing it to the Dean.

“What’s this?” Dean Withers scowled as she took the disk. “A mini-disk? You are supposed to be using micro-disks at your age.”

“Yes, ma’am.” Elariel was back to stammering, her left eye twitching furiously as she tried to explain her way out of trouble. “It’s just that…”

“You say that a lot, child,” the Dean scolded. “Take a pause and make yourself boardroom ready. You should have learned that in your ninth year!”

“My apologies, ma’am.” Elariel forced herself to regain control of the meeting. “The speech is on a mini instead of a micro because I only decided to ask your help last night, ma’am. I…” She paused. “I am out of micro-disks, but I had a box of these in storage from my eighth year, so I chose to take the chance of suffering your disapproval in order to gain the safety of your proofreading my speech, ma’am.”

“Well-presented arguments.” Dean Withers’ expression turned soft, wondering how Elariel was out of micro-disks as she slid the mini-disk into her laptop. “What do your parents do for work, if I may ask?”

Elariel was completely caught off guard by the sudden shift in the conversation.

“I’m sorry?”

“May I ask where your parents work?” Dean Withers repeated.

“My mother is a Branch Manager at Bank three-two-seven, ma’am.”

Dean Withers’ expression soured for just a beat. “And your father?”

Elariel’s eyes dropped to her toes as a tear welled up in her eye. “We lost him when I was in my ninth year,” she choked back a sob.

“I’m so sorry, my dear.” The Dean reached across her desk and put her hand over Elariel’s. “Let’s take a look at this speech, ok?”

“Thank you, ma’am.”

“May I ask what he did for work?”

“Father worked for Rubix Corporation,” she said proudly. “He was the lead researcher using Continuum Lattice’s natural cooling properties as refrigeration.”

“He was on the team that developed the Lattice Cooler?”

“Yes, ma’am!” Elariel smiled. “Unfortunately during beta testing for the first prototype. They were miniaturizing the thermal multipliers embedded in the cooler walls, but…” She paused at the pain of the memory. “The math was wrong.” She stammered as she wiped a tear. “His engineer did not properly account for the magnification curve created by miniaturizing the thermal multipliers. The moment they activated the Lattice, the multipliers overcharged and exploded.”

“That sounds horrible,” Dean Withers said softly.

“Everyone in the lab was frozen in a single microsecond,” Elariel looked up, a smile replacing the tears as another memory flowed into her mind. “They entombed him in Lattice and placed him in the Hall of Heroes in the Corporate Headquarters Museum, with a plaque describing the breakthrough technology that his research pioneered. Mom and I are allowed free entry, so I go and talk to him quite often.”

“A one-sided conversation that leaves you feeling encouraged?” The Dean smiled.

Elariel was silent as the Dean returned her attention to the screen, her expressions washing through emotions, from pride to unexpected revelation, as she reached the part Elariel’s mother was worried about.

“This is…” Her voice trailed off for a moment before she cleared her throat and tried again. “This is not just a valedictorian speech.” She locked eyes with Elariel. “This is a call to action!”

“Thank you,” Elariel said shyly.

“But I do see why your mother was concerned.” She took a sudden clinical tone as she explained. “However, the concern isn’t your thinking, Elariel. It’s your framing. When you write ‘we called it rulers… we called it oppression,’ ‘called’ is a declaration. It asserts a shared reality. It goes into a transcript as a statement of fact about the Union. Do you understand?”

Elariel nodded slowly.

“’We thought,’ on the other hand — ‘we thought it was rulers, we thought it was oppression’ — that’s interior. That’s a young mind working through an idea. Reflective. Metaphoric, even. The distinction is two words on paper and an ocean in a hearing room.”

“I understand.” Even as realization dawned, Elariel’s mind had already begun planning the minor changes to her speech and envisioning a cheering crowd after delivering it. “And I will never forget that advice. It seems it would apply equally to several aspects of life.”

“Exactly.” The Dean smiled as she reached into her desk drawer, pulling out a cartridge with ten micro-disks. “And here, take these.”

“Oh, ma’am, I couldn’t.” Elariel knew the value of micro-disks, especially an entire cartridge of them. “Maybe just one, but…”

“Nonsense!” Dean Withers laughed softly. “The one thing we fail to teach our students in this education system is humility. We train you to be proud, confident, and financially responsible, but we never teach our students to accept a gift that they cannot afford. Take these. You will need more than this before you finish University, but it will get you started. I already planned to give it to you, by the way.” She paused as she stuffed the cartridge in Elariel’s hand. “I will not be denied the chance to give what little I have to offer to the brightest mind of our century.”

Elariel smiled as she accepted the generous gift.

The next month passed so fast Elariel felt like she had missed it altogether. Her days were filled with ceremony practice, public speaking coaching and, as always, more research surrounding her speech. When graduation day finally came and she was called to the stage to deliver her keynote speech, she was surprised by the sense of calm all of the extra training had created in her. Usually, when preparing to speak in front of faculty and Monitors, she found herself preparing by vomiting the contents of her stomach moments before taking the microphone.

Today was different as she stepped into center stage. “Welcome, fellow graduates, friends and families of graduates. We hope you have found the ceremony so far efficient and pleasing, and we will do our best to make sure you enjoy your day with us.” Her delivery was clinical, precise, and impressive.

She started reading the speech as the teleprompter scrolled across the transparent display, reading it exactly as she had crafted it to be read, not missing a single emotional beat.

She finally came to the end. “In Primary school we thought they were rulers, in secondary, we thought we were oppressed by the rules, in tertiary school we learned the consequences of our behavior. And we began thinking it might be an Oligarchy. But today I suggest we call it by its real name, The Union of the Five, and that we give this system, within which we are mere cogs at this age, the credit it deserves, because it is really none of these. This governmental system our founders have forged is the gold standard example of a true Meritocracy!”

Applause erupted through the auditorium, students, faculty, and Monitors alike, all cheering, jumping to their feet with the ovation.
